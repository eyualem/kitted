# Real outfit photos in the style game: where they can come from

**Date:** 2026-11-19 · **Asked by:** the founder (via the control session) · **Cloud copy:** none yet
**What this is:** research only. No app code, no spec changed, nothing downloaded, nothing bought, no account made. Every license quote below was read on the source's own live page on Nov 19 with the in-app browser.

## The answer in one paragraph
**Yes, the game can show real photos, and it should, but not from Pinterest.** Pinterest's pictures belong to whoever uploaded them, its terms give no right to reuse a pin in another app, and its developer rules forbid exactly what a swipe card would do. The best source is **our own shoot of 16 outfits, shown from the neck down, with a signed release from every person (and a written OK from whoever presses the shutter)**: about $0–100 and half a day of the founder's time, and it is the only option where every photo reads as its look, the lighting matches across all 8 looks, and the people's permission is on paper. If he wants photos in the very next TestFlight build, **free stock from Unsplash (then Pexels), bundled in the app and cropped from the neck down,** is a $0 bridge that is legal to ship but carries one gap we cannot close: the site does not promise the people in the photo agreed. The AI-generated route works legally but isn't "real photos", and I'd advise against it. **The one decision for the founder: shoot our own 16 (recommended), or accept the stock bridge for now?**

### Pinterest (what the founder suggested): no
- **The photos aren't Pinterest's.** Terms of Service §3(a): "You retain all rights in, and are solely responsible for, the User Content you post." The license a poster gives (§3(b)) is to Pinterest "and our users" to use, display and redistribute the content "for the purpose of promoting and redistributing part or all of the Pinterest Service". That is a license to use pins on Pinterest, not a grant to put them in another company's paid app. And §3(a) only asks posters to post what "you have the rights to post", so many pins are other people's copyrighted photos (Pinterest's own Copyright page is a takedown process, "in keeping with the Digital Millennium Copyright Act").
- **No scraping:** §2(a): "you agree not to scrape, collect, search, copy or otherwise access data or content from Pinterest in unauthorized ways, such as by using automated means".
- **The official API is built for the opposite:** Developer guidelines: "you may not store any information accessed through any Pinterest Materials including the API. Instead, call the API each time you need to access information." An app that shows Pins "must: Link Pins back to their source on Pinterest... Not cover or obscure content from Pinterest. For example, don't apply filters to Pins. Not create new content from Pins that can be distributed on your app or service." A neck-down crop on a swipe card would break the second and third rules.
- **Apple would ask.** App Review Guidelines 5.2.1: "Don't use protected third-party material such as trademarks, copyrighted works, or patented ideas in your app without permission." 5.2.2: if an app "displays content from a third-party service, ensure that you are specifically permitted to do so under the service's terms of use. Authorization must be provided upon request."
- Links: [Terms](https://policy.pinterest.com/en/terms-of-service) · [Developer guidelines](https://policy.pinterest.com/en/developer-guidelines) · [Copyright](https://policy.pinterest.com/en/copyright) · [Apple 5.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- *Pinterest's separate Developer and API Terms page (developers.pinterest.com/terms) didn't render for me, so the above rests on the Terms of Service and Developer guidelines, which are the pages that matter here.*

### Unsplash
- **License** ([unsplash.com/license](https://unsplash.com/license)): "Unsplash grants you an irrevocable, nonexclusive, worldwide copyright license to download, copy, modify, distribute, perform, and use images from Unsplash for free, including for commercial purposes, without permission from or attributing the photographer or Unsplash. This license does not include the right to compile images from Unsplash to replicate a similar or competing service." Not permitted: "Images cannot be sold without significant modification."
- **Bundling:** fine when we download the picture ourselves (the license says "download, copy, modify, distribute"). The **API is the other way round** ([API Terms](https://unsplash.com/api-terms)): "you must directly use or embed the related image URLs returned by the API in your Developer Apps (generally referred to as 'hotlinking')", plus a download-notice call. We don't want the API; a game card must open offline and the same each time.
- **Attribution:** not required ("appreciated").
- **People's likeness: not covered.** [Terms](https://unsplash.com/terms): "the Unsplash License does not include the right to use: Trademarks, logos, or brands that appear in Images; People's images if they are recognizable in the Images; Works of art or authorship that appear in Images." Uploaders promise they hold releases ([help article](https://help.unsplash.com/en/articles/2612326-can-i-use-images-of-landmarks-and-notable-buildings): "the contributor agrees that they have the proper releases (model, trademark, etc.)"), but Unsplash passes none of that on to us.
- **Competing-service clause:** "replicate a similar or competing service" means another photo library. A fixed set of 16 pictures in a style quiz isn't one.
- *Precedent in this repo:* Welcome already uses an Unsplash photo with the face cropped out (`specs/onboarding-screens.md`, line 11), accepting the same residual risk.

### Pexels
- **License** ([pexels.com/license](https://www.pexels.com/license/)): "All photos and videos on Pexels can be downloaded and used for free." Allowed: "On your website, blog or app". "Attribution is not required." "You can modify the photos and videos from Pexels." Not allowed: "Identifiable people may not appear in a bad light or in a way that is offensive"; "Don't imply endorsement of your product by people or brands on the imagery"; "Don't redistribute or sell the photos and videos on other stock photo or wallpaper platforms."
- **People: not covered** ([Terms of Use](https://www.pexels.com/terms-of-service/)): "Responsibility for determining whether permissions are needed always rests solely and exclusively with you. We do not warrant that any consents or licenses have been obtained in relation to any Content, and we expressly disclaim any and all responsibility and liability in relation to such matters." It adds that for commercial use "it is likely that you will need consent or a license" where the content shows "identifiable people, logos, brands of all kinds".
- **Competing service:** Terms: "use or compile any Content to replicate a similar or competing service to the Service". The API docs add "You may not copy or replicate core functionality of Pexels (including making Pexels content available as a wallpaper app)". The API is hotlink-based and wants visible attribution; we'd download instead ([API docs](https://www.pexels.com/api/documentation/)).
- *Implication of "endorsement":* a guy's photo on a card saying "Is this your style?" doesn't say he endorses Kitted, but this is another reason to crop faces and avoid brands.

### Pixabay
- **License** ([summary](https://pixabay.com/service/license-summary/); only the full license is binding): free use, "without having to attribute the author", "Modify or adapt Content into new works". Prohibited: standalone sale or distribution; recognizable trademarks "for commercial purposes in relation to goods and services"; "You cannot use Content in any immoral or illegal way, especially Content which features recognisable people."
- **People:** [Terms](https://pixabay.com/service/terms/) carry the same "identifiable people... you must consider whether you require the consent of a third party" language as Pexels (same text, it's now a Canva brand).
- **Bundling:** the API docs ([pixabay.com/api/docs](https://pixabay.com/api/docs/)): "permanent hotlinking of images (using Pixabay URLs in your app) is not allowed. If you intend to use the images, please download them to your server first." So download-and-bundle is the supported route.
- Mostly illustration and lifestyle; I'd expect the fewest usable outfit photos. I did not search it.

### Paid stock
- **iStock** ([license agreement](https://www.istockphoto.com/legal/license-agreement)): examples of allowed use include "web and mobile applications". **Releases are warranted:** "your use of the content in accordance with this agreement and in the form delivered by iStock will not infringe... or violate any right of privacy or right of publicity; and all necessary model and/or property releases for use of the content in the manner authorized by this agreement have been obtained. Note that you are solely responsible for any edits made to the content." Editorial-only pictures "may not be used for any commercial... purpose". **Catch:** the warranty covers the picture "in the form delivered". A neck-down crop is our edit. The release still exists, but the warranty wording doesn't clearly cover the cropped version. Getty's EULA ([gettyimages.com/eula](https://www.gettyimages.com/eula)) has the same shape ("excluding any modifications, overlays or re‑focusing done by you" and only "where a model release has been obtained").
- **Price on its plans page** ([istockphoto.com/plans-and-pricing](https://www.istockphoto.com/plans-and-pricing)): Basic $29/month for 10 downloads ($2.90 each) and Premium $70/month ($7.00 each), both quoted on the annual plan; a footnote prices a single credit at $12.00. I did not open the month-to-month or credit-pack prices, so 16 photos is **roughly $60–190** depending on plan. Read the cancellation terms before any purchase.
- **Adobe Stock** ([license terms](https://stock.adobe.com/license-terms)): the Standard license lets you "Reproduce up to 500,000 copies of the asset in all media, including product packaging, printed marketing materials, digital documents, or software", with no stand-alone redistribution; above 500,000 copies you need an Enhanced license (not offered for ordinary photos), so a successful app would outgrow it. Plans ([stock.adobe.com/plans](https://stock.adobe.com/plans)): 10 credits a month for $29.99 on an annual plan billed monthly, with "a fee... up to half your remaining annual commitment if you cancel after 14 days"; the page title offers 10 free images on a trial. **Not verified:** Adobe's model-release warranty (the Terms link, `adobe.com/go/stockterms`, answered with a file download instead of a page; I did not retry it, and no file was saved by me).
- **Shutterstock:** its license page returned HTTP 403 to the browser, so I did not read it and haven't stated its terms.
- Downsides of paid stock for this job: the same photo can show up in a competitor's app or an ad; clothes in stock photos are staged for models, not tuned to read as "Workwear" vs "Outdoor"; and the picture search is the same effort as for free sites.

### Our own shoot
- **What it needs on paper:** (a) a signed release from each person pictured, 18 or older, saying the app may show the photo (cropped or not) worldwide, in the app, App Store pages and marketing, with no pay and no end date; (b) a written license or assignment from whoever takes the photos. Copyright belongs to the photographer by default, even if a friend; "my friend shot it" isn't enough. (c) Brand logos kept out of frame.
- This is also exactly what the spec's "Later" already says: "Real outfit photos in the game, with the people's written OK."
- Not legal advice: have a one-page release checked once (a friend who's a law student, a template from a photography association). I can draft the first version if he says so.

### AI-generated images
- **Ownership:** OpenAI's terms ([terms of use](https://openai.com/policies/terms-of-use/)): "you (a) retain your ownership rights in Input and (b) own the Output... to the extent permitted by applicable law", and "output may not be unique and other users may receive similar output". The US Copyright Office's Part 2 report (Jan 2025, [press release](https://www.copyright.gov/newsnet/2025/1060.html)): generative AI outputs "can be protected by copyright only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements... but not the mere provision of prompts." So we could use them, but nobody (including us) could stop others from copying them.
- **People:** a generated person isn't a real person, so there's no release to get, though a face can resemble someone real.
- **Would the founder want it? Probably not.** His ask is "real photos instead of drawings so the looks are easier to understand", and an image generator invents the clothes: seams, collars and proportions that no real shirt has, which defeats "easier to understand" and clashes with "the AI sees, the code judges". It would also be the one place the app shows something that isn't real, in an app whose pitch is honest feedback. It's a quick fallback; I'd only suggest it if he explicitly prefers speed over realness.

## 2. Fit with Kitted's rules
- **"Never rate faces, bodies, weight or skin."** The photos show other people, never the user, and nothing rates them. The rule is about the app's *output*, so it isn't broken. But the game's promise to look past the person is better kept if the picture is about the clothes: **recommend a neck-down crop on every card**, matching how the app already treats faces (the face is never sent, and Welcome's photo is cropped the same way). It also takes away the biggest risk in section 1 (a recognizable person) and the biggest fairness bias below (a good-looking face pulling a "Yes"). Keep hands and shoes in frame if they carry the look.
- **Hard line: nothing about a body.** Don't pick photos for or against a body type. Do show a range of builds and skin tones across the 16 (hands and forearms still show skin after the crop), and **deal the people to the looks the way the game deals the cards** (not "the same guy is always Streetwear"). Use two different people per look.
- **Spec change needed:** `specs/style-game.md` says "never a person in the drawings" (Rules) and lists "real photos in the game (drawings only)" as out of scope. This changes only with his yes.
- **Audience and readability.** 18–30 US guys; clothes that look like what a 22-year-old would wear; each photo has to read as its look *at card size*, with top, bottom and shoes visible (as the drawings do), no logos (stock search results include things like a pullover with a big brand name), plain background, front-facing or three-quarter, daylight.
- **Count:** 16 photos (2 per look × 8): 8 first-card photos and 8 second-outfit photos (SG1, SG7 FS1). Plus every other place the same drawings show (Welcome, Add a look, the results rows, the ranking, Style profile tiles): spec says Style profile keeps its tiles, so either the photos go only into the game and results, or all drawings are swapped. That's a small design call for the spec update.
- **Free stock can't be controlled for fit.** Full-length, clean, logo-free, men's, readable-as-one-look photos are rare. Expect to page through 20+ candidates per look. On a shoot, he chooses the clothes: use each drawing's piece list in `looks.ts` as the shopping list.

## 4. Ranked recommendation
1. **Own shoot, neck-down, with signed releases.** Best on every rule: permission on paper, one look and light across all 8 looks (so no look gets a prettier model or better backdrop), outfits chosen to read, and it matches what the spec's "Later" already promised. Cost is his half-day. Rank: **#1**.
2. **Free stock bridge (Unsplash first, Pexels second), bundled, neck-down, no brands, logged by photo URL and date** (so any one can be swapped out in a day if a photographer or person ever objects). Ships soonest at $0, but we'd be shipping 16 people whose agreement nobody promised us. Use only if he wants photos in the next build, and replace with the shoot later. Rank: **#2**.
3. **Paid stock (iStock first).** The only stock route with a release warranty, but the crop weakens it, it costs real money, and it still needs the same hunt. Rank: **#3**.
4. **AI images.** Legal and cheap, not real, can't be owned. Rank: **#4**, not advised.
5. **Pinterest.** Rule out.

### Do the fairness numbers need re-checking? Yes
`tasks/style-quiz/quiz_sim.py` (and `flow_sim.py`, which imports its drawing table) rest on judgments of the **drawings**: `DRAWINGS_AFTER` gives each of the 16 drawings a "leak" (how much it reads as a neighbouring look) and a "pull" (how loud it is), plus one shared `OUTFIT_SD` of 0.15 for how differently people feel about a drawing. Photos change all three:
- **Leak and pull must be re-estimated per photo.** A photo carries a lot more than the outfit (a sweater in a studio vs a hoodie on a street, the colour of the wall, the pose). Each of the 16 gets a new number.
- **OUTFIT_SD probably goes up:** people react to the person, place and light. Run the sim at 0.15 and at 0.20–0.25 as a stress test.
- **A cheap $0 way to get real numbers:** show the 16 photos (shuffled, no names) to ~10 guys aged 18–30 in a form and ask "which of these 8 looks is it?" (gives the leak matrix) and "would you wear it?" (gives pull). Then put those values into `DRAWINGS_AFTER`, rerun both sims (about 2 minutes for the full `quiz_sim.py`) and compare to today's 91% top-3 and 2–3% disliked-look figures.
- On a shoot, the same lighting and framing for all 16 should make pull close to equal by design, which is a further reason it ranks first.
