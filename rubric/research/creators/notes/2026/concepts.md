# Fourteen style concepts, studied before they become rules (T95)

Written Nov 14, 2026, for the rulebook the new scoring is tuned on. For each concept: what it says, how to see it in one full-body photo, where it bends, how it changes the others, what the four creators said (video id and [mm:ss], quotes of 12 words or fewer, exact from the transcript), and whether the app's AI reading can see it today.

**Scope.** Clothes only, and how they relate to each other. Where a concept is usually taught with bodies ("long legs", "short torso", "flattering on your frame"), it is re-stated in clothing terms: where a hem ends against the trouser waistband, how wide a leg is against the top, and so on. Advice about skin tone, undertone or "what suits your colouring" (common on the web for concepts 6 and 8) is left out on purpose.

**Who is who.** TD = Tim Dessaint, WK = Wanhee Kim, JA = Jaye Alexander, FA = Frugal Aesthetic. Transcripts are YouTube's own captions, so a few words are garbled; those are marked "(caption unclear)". `[music]` tags inside a quote are dropped.

**How sure this is.** The transcripts were searched by keyword across all 66 kept videos of the four, and the passages that define each idea were read in full. Not every video was read end to end, so a passing mention can be missing. The web sources are mostly 2024–2026 style journalism and retailer guides. There is no research-grade source on any of these, so treat the web material as consensus among stylists, not fact.

## 1. The rule of thirds (and the golden-ratio version)

**In plain words.** Split the outfit into two blocks of unequal length, about one third and two thirds, instead of two equal halves. Usually the top is the short block and the trousers the long one; the reverse (a long coat or long top over a short visible bottom) also works. The golden-ratio version (1 : 1.618, about 38/62) is the same idea with a less extreme split. The common point of both: **not 50/50.**

**How to see it in a photo.** Ignore the person and measure the clothes. Take the top block from the collar to the lowest visible hem of the top layer (or the tuck line), and the bottom block from there to where the trousers end on the shoe. If the top block is clearly shorter than the bottom block (about 1:2), it passes. If the top ends about halfway between collar and trouser hem (a long untucked tee or a hip-length jacket over a low-rise trouser), that is the "cut in half" look the creators warn about. An open jacket makes two lines; the eye reads the inner top's hem and the jacket's hem together, so the jacket's length matters most when it is closed.

**Where it bends.**
- The long-top version is a look in its own right, not a failure: WK shows a low, long split and says it "doesn't look that bad" (RtxJqd5Drps [01:40]). Long coats over everything are a deliberate 2/3-on-top split.
- Streetwear "big tee, big pants" often sits near 50/50 on purpose. It works when the volume is matched and the hem lands on the shoe, and fails when the trousers are slim.
- Tailoring gets the thirds for free: a jacket ending near the bottom of the trouser seat over a mid- or high-rise trouser.
- FA (If6OJBbi_-8 [17:04]) ranks it as a useful guide but calls it a cop-out to treat it as the whole answer: it is "a good visual guide to fit building content creator copout". In other words, a guideline, not a law.
- The phrase is overloaded. JA's rule of thirds (N0nN17cwVqo, a dropped video, and AadxlP3kEmI [24:27]) is about **framing the photo** on a 3x3 grid, not about the outfit. The rulebook must not mix them up.

**How it interacts.**
- With 2 (waist break): the tuck, crop or at-belt hem is *how* you get the thirds. They are one idea seen from two sides.
- With 13 (hem): a pooling hem shortens the bottom block visually and pulls the split back toward 50/50; a clean slight break keeps the long block long.
- With 6 (value contrast): the split is only visible if the top and bottom differ in lightness. Tonal or all-black outfits hide the line, so the thirds matter less there, and the outline (5) takes over (FA Vn6KlXkU2xA [09:16]).
- With 4 (balance): a short top block over wide trousers is the A-shape that FA and the "big pants, small top" trend favour.

**What the four say.**
- WK, the clearest: "a third top, 2/3 at the bottom" (RtxJqd5Drps [01:04]); then shows tucking in and pulling the trouser up versus wearing it low and long, and closes with "It's about the proportions that you're creating." (RtxJqd5Drps [01:40]). Earlier, borrowed from photography: "one third top and then two thirds" (ew3gCk1zkso [11:15]), with the advice to tuck the tee and avoid long flannels that fall low.
- FA: defines it from photography, "give things more room to breathe" (k6Gig6r7BSQ [05:44]), and says the crop "breaks your body down into proportionate sections" (k6Gig6r7BSQ [06:14]); calls it a good guide, not the whole truth (If6OJBbi_-8 [17:04]).
- TD: never says "thirds", but asks the same question: "Where should your tops end?" and "There is no universally correct answer." (0ilmLux6mPg [08:51]). He praises a boxy jacket whose hem lands where the trouser waist begins: "the jacket cuts abruptly where the trouser starts" (0ilmLux6mPg [08:15]).
- JA: "I would recommend using the rule of thirds right" (23ZCHsp9AZU [05:04]), in passing; his longer uses are about photos.
- **Agreement:** all four favour an unequal split; WK and TD say the short-top version is not the only right answer. No one gives numbers.

**Can the AI see it today?** Partly. `length` (cropped / at_belt / mid_fly / below_crotch) plus `tucked` give where the top ends. Missing: the trouser rise (a low rise moves the line down), and a reliable long-top read (`below_crotch` almost never fires, so a long top can be mistaken for a mid-fly one). A jacket's length is read per garment, and `shows_below_outer` says whether an inner layer peeks out, which helps with the two-line case.

## 2. The waist break (visual waist: tuck, crop, at-belt hem)

**In plain words.** A visible horizontal line at the trouser waistband, where the top stops and the trousers start. It is made by tucking (full, half or French tuck), by a cropped top or jacket, or by a top whose hem lands right at the belt. It gives the outfit two clean blocks instead of one long tube, and it is the mechanism behind concept 1.

**How to see it in a photo.** Look at the trouser waistband. Can you see it, or a belt, or a tuck line? Does the top's hem end at it, or within a hand's width of it? Or does the top hang to mid-fly or below and hide the waistband entirely? With an open jacket, check the inner top. A belt in a contrasting colour makes the line sharper; a top and trouser of the same colour make it softer.

**Where it bends.**
- Too much crop becomes its own trend look. WK dislikes "super cropped boxy" tees (Mr-GzwGQ1qY [03:32]) and wants a tee that ends at the belt without being a crop top. FA: "you don't need as much crop as you think" (TnNv7Zvui6E [10:15]), said about trousers, with the same logic.
- Some styles skip it on purpose: long tees and hoodies over wide trousers in streetwear, long overshirts in workwear, long coats. There the outline (5) and the hem (13) carry the outfit.
- A tuck changes formality. TD likes an oversized tee tucked into tailored trousers as a smart-casual balance (-V2bE8nDsdo [04:37]); a tucked tee in sweatpants reads odd.
- A tuck needs a trouser with a defined waist (tailored, high-waisted, belted). FA: "just tight waist wide legs" (XtcY-zmoS-E [04:07]).

**How it interacts.**
- With 4 and 5: a waist line plus volume above and below makes the X or hourglass shape (FA's tight-waist wide trousers; JA's flares).
- With 14 (layering): a cropped jacket or a cinched hoodie can make the waist line over a long base layer (FA cinches a hoodie to layer it, Jh8dz-KZcfY [00:34]; TD's slightly cropped jacket, wIib9hzVUuw [04:35]).
- With 6: the line is only as strong as the lightness gap between top and bottom.

**What the four say.**
- FA (most): the crop "creates more space between your top and your pants" (k6Gig6r7BSQ [06:14]); a fitted waist with a wide leg is an "hourglass situation creates separation from my top and my bottom" (XtcY-zmoS-E [04:38]–[05:08]); cropped shirts are "great for creating separation from your top and and your bottom" (TnNv7Zvui6E [01:03]); at-home test: fold the shirt up until it sits at the waist and compare photos (k6Gig6r7BSQ [09:20]–[10:22]).
- TD: "oversized t-shirt tucked into the tailored trousers" (-V2bE8nDsdo [04:37]); a tee that will "tuck into the pants" fixes a too-long layered look (wIib9hzVUuw [09:40]); a "slightly cropped jacket" adds interest to a plain monochrome outfit (XIYvycsZxDk [03:25]).
- WK: imagines "if this thing was tucked in" with the trouser worn higher (RtxJqd5Drps [01:04]); his ideal tee ends at the belt but is "not too cropped like those fashion influencers" (Mr-GzwGQ1qY [06:04]).
- JA: over a leather jacket, "A crop t-shirt is probably going to look better." (Jz9QVWgBbOg [23:49]); high-rise trousers with a tuck or a crop are harder to pull off (RIuzSb8OLMM [14:14]).
- **Agreement:** all four. The split is how much crop: FA and JA like cropped pieces; WK and TD prefer an at-belt hem or a tuck over a true crop.

**Can the AI see it today?** Partly. `tucked` and `length` = cropped / at_belt cover the main cases. Not read: half or French tuck (unless `tucked` is a partial value; check the schema), a belt as a separate item, and the trouser rise. `length` is weak at the long end, so "no waist break" is under-detected.

## 3. Visual weight (what makes a piece read heavy)

**In plain words.** How strongly a piece pulls the eye and "sits" in the picture. From design theory: bigger, darker, more saturated, more textured, more patterned and denser elements weigh more (Smashing Magazine, 2014, the standard explainer). In clothes this means dark or bright colours, large volume, chunky knits, bold or large-scale patterns, logos, and chunky shoes or boots. Weight is relative: a white sneaker is heavy under all-black, light under white trousers.

**How to see it in a photo.** Squint until details blur. What stays visible longest? Which half has more of it? Check each heavy feature: the darkest piece, the brightest piece, the widest piece, any large pattern or logo, chunky knits or puffers, and the size of the shoes against the trouser hem.

**Where it bends.**
- A heavy element is fine, even good, when it is the focal point on purpose (12). Trouble comes from two or three heavy things competing, or one landing at the wrong end.
- Dark shoes "ground" an outfit; light shoes under dark trousers lift the eye to the feet. TD uses this deliberately: he matches black loafers to black trousers so the eye goes up (wIib9hzVUuw [08:07]).
- All-black removes colour weight, so the outline (5) and texture (14) carry all of it (FA Vn6KlXkU2xA [09:16]).

**How it interacts.** It is the shared currency of 4 (where the weight sits), 6 (darkness), 7 (brightness), 12 (accent = a small, deliberate heavy spot), 13 (chunky shoe against the hem) and 14 (layers heavier outward). Most of the "two parts change each other" examples in the rulebook are weight trades.

**What the four say.**
- TD, by name: "visual weight on the upper body or the lower body" is a preference to know, with no single right answer (0ilmLux6mPg [08:51]); "it's quite a topheavy outfit with the chunky oversized scarf" (wIib9hzVUuw [05:36]), fixed by lighter, relaxed jeans and a plain scarf; "heavyweight chunky knitwear adds that lovely texture and weight to an outfit" (-e9Is50-fn0 [08:16]); heavier layers go outside, or "the balance in your look would feel off" (IUMqpLeCT-E [03:15]).
- FA: "a more solid color creates a more defined shape" (TnNv7Zvui6E [10:45]), so a flat dark trouser shows its outline more than a faded one; bulky shoes want bigger trousers: "the contrast between your big shoes and your pants always works" (8rmkOpCbFTM [11:02]).
- WK: a dark cap and dark shoes "contain" a bright outfit: "use Caps or hats and shoes as a way to contain" (rNCrnP-os0U [06:10]); bright pieces should be surrounded with dark ones (tNVdA_arEa8 [00:31]).
- JA: "Oversized jackets, they need balance." (Jz9QVWgBbOg [13:41]); layering without a system makes outfits feel "bulky, messy, or just off" (RIuzSb8OLMM [09:11]).
- **Agreement:** all four use the idea; only TD names it. No one gives a weight scale.

**Can the AI see it today?** Partly. `volume` (weak at 4–5), `saturation`, `pattern` + `pattern_scale`, `logo`, and the shoe `subcategory` cover size, brightness and busyness. Not read: lightness (value) and texture, the two biggest weight factors after size. Lightness can be estimated from `color_family` (see 6).

## 4. Top-heavy vs bottom-heavy, and balance

**In plain words.** Where the weight (3) and the width sit, top half against bottom half, shoes included. "Balance" in the creators' mouths mostly means **one loose half, one fitted half**, or matched volumes on purpose. A top-heavy outfit has all the volume, darkness or busyness up top over thin, light or short trousers. A bottom-heavy outfit has a small top over wide, dark trousers and heavy shoes, which right now is mostly read as *good* (the A-shape).

**How to see it in a photo.** Compare the width of the top at its widest with the width of the trousers at their widest (FA's test, below). Then compare lightness and busyness top against bottom. Then look at the shoes: do they match the trouser volume (chunky under wide, slim under slim)?

**Where it bends.**
- Fashion swings. 2024–2026 menswear coverage calls "big pants, small top" the go-to formula (Mr Porter, Harper's Bazaar). Harper's Bazaar India (Aug 2025) argues for the opposite, an oversized top over slim trousers, citing Celine, Chloé and Prada. Wide-top, slim-bottom is a real style (Rick Owens, some streetwear, 2010s skinny-jeans looks), not an error.
- JA says skinny jeans with big runners and a boxy tee is a valid style even though he no longer wears it (sOfjDjtjBWM [08:48]).
- Matched big-on-big works when it is clearly deliberate and the hems are controlled; Man of Many's stylist warns that when both halves billow "you lose the silhouette".
- A dark or chunky shoe can rescue a top-heavy outfit by adding weight below.

**How it interacts.** With 5 (it *is* the silhouette question), 13 (a wide leg needs enough length on the shoe; a pooled slim leg looks heavy for the wrong reason), 3 and 6 (a dark top over light trousers is top-heavy even when the volumes match), 14 (each added layer adds top weight; in summer there is nothing to add, so the trouser shape does the work).

**What the four say.**
- FA, the clearest rule: "make sure your top is the same width if not Slimmer" than the widest part of the trousers (XtcY-zmoS-E [02:04]); "the big pants, small top ensemble, just polarization" (xL1NeaA9D-Y [04:18]); for all-black, "a fairly fitted top with a relaxed bottom" (Vn6KlXkU2xA [10:19]).
- TD: no single rule: "There is no universally correct answer." (0ilmLux6mPg [08:51]); but he fixes a top-heavy look by moving weight down (wIib9hzVUuw [05:36]) and fixes tight-on-tight by loosening both (wIib9hzVUuw [09:10]).
- WK: "normal fitting or slightly loose fitting" for most; too oversized enters "another territory" (rNCrnP-os0U [08:12]).
- JA: balance is his main word ("Fashion is all about balance.", VUEaMYx10mM [11:27]) and covers proportion, accessories and statement-versus-basic; a leather jacket over a bulky shirt fails: "your shirt too bulky, your jacket too bulky" (Jz9QVWgBbOg [23:19]).
- **Agreement:** all four want one half to answer the other. FA is the only one with a directional rule (top no wider than the trousers). TD and JA say the direction is a choice. This is a real split to settle with the founder: is wide-top, slim-bottom a fault or a style?

**Can the AI see it today?** Partly. Top `volume` against bottom `leg_shape` gives the width comparison, but `volume` 4–5 and slim vs straight are both weak reads, which is exactly where this rule lives. Shoe bulk is only in `subcategory`. Top-versus-bottom lightness needs the value lookup (6).

## 5. The classic silhouettes in menswear

**In plain words.** The outline of the outfit with all colour removed. The letter names come from pattern-making and runway language:
- **A (triangle):** narrower on top, wider at the bottom: fitted or cropped top, wide or flared trousers. Today's "big pants, small top".
- **V or Y (inverted triangle):** wide on top, narrow at the bottom: big jacket, hoodie or puffer over slim or skinny trousers.
- **H or I (column):** straight and even from top to bottom: straight trousers, an un-tucked straight top or a long straight coat. Clean, tailored, minimalist.
- **X (hourglass):** wide on top, defined at the waistband, wide at the hem: a full top tucked or cropped into a high, fitted waist with wide or flared legs. JA's flared-denim looks, FA's "tight waist wide legs".
- **O:** round, volume in the middle: oversized knits, balloon trousers, Japanese "city boy" layering.

**How to see it in a photo.** Trace the outer edge of the clothes from the collar to the shoes: wide or narrow at the shoulders of the garment, at the waistband, at the knee, at the hem. Then name the letter. Two checks matter more than the letter: is the shape **deliberate** (every piece supports it), and do the **shoes** match it (chunky shoes under A and X; slim shoes under H and V)?

**Where it bends.**
- WK warns against using the letters as a checklist: "don't get caught up in all of that" (rNCrnP-os0U [07:41]). Most good everyday outfits are a soft A or H.
- Silhouettes date. TD: proportions shift and today's classic can look dated in five years (poTk87gGHtg [02:04]).
- A V shape with skinny jeans reads 2015 today, but a V made with a structured jacket over a straight trouser still reads modern.

**How it interacts.** With 1 and 2 (where the outline narrows is the waist line), 4 (A vs V is bottom-heavy vs top-heavy), 13 (the hem finishes the outline; a flare needs length on the shoe), 6 (low contrast and all-black make the outline the main event), 14 (the outer layer sets the outline: JA, below).

**What the four say.**
- WK: "if you were to take all the color out of it" it is "just the shape of your outfit" (tNVdA_arEa8 [05:09]); lists "an O shaped silhouette an a shaped silhouette a v-shaped i-shaped" and says not to get stuck on them (rNCrnP-os0U [07:41]); mentions the O shape of "city boy" style (ew3gCk1zkso [10:42]).
- JA: flares make the X: "The pants have a flare. So, this creates an hourglass silhouette." (Pxeeh-caTJg [22:13]); "the outer layer defines silhouette and the tone of the fit" (RIuzSb8OLMM [10:11]); short outer layers feel easy, long ones "create presence".
- TD: "Your preferred silhouette is arguably more important than your preferred aesthetic" (0ilmLux6mPg [08:15]); a boxy top half against tailored trousers is what makes an outfit work, not the pieces themselves; a long coat makes "one continuous controlled shape" (0ilmLux6mPg [08:51]).
- FA: the A ("big pants, small top", xL1NeaA9D-Y [04:18]) and the X ("hourglass situation", XtcY-zmoS-E [04:38]); in all-black, "your entire silhouette is completely exaggerated" (Vn6KlXkU2xA [09:16]).
- **Agreement:** all four put the outline above the colour. A, X and H are praised; V is mentioned only as something to avoid with tight pieces (WK on skinny jeans and a tight tee, tNVdA_arEa8 [05:40]).

**Can the AI see it today?** Partly, by combining fields: top `volume` + `length` + `tucked`, bottom `leg_shape` (flare_bootcut and barrel exist, good for A and X), shoe `subcategory`. No direct "outline" read, and the weak reads (`volume` 4–5, slim vs straight) blur the line between H and A or H and V.

## 6. Value contrast (light against dark between pieces)

**In plain words.** "Value" is lightness, separate from hue and from saturation. Value contrast is how far apart in lightness the pieces are.
- **High contrast** (white tee, black trousers; navy suit, white shirt): crisp, graphic, more formal or more striking.
- **Medium contrast** (light-blue jeans with a navy top; cream with olive): relaxed, easy, the everyday default.
- **Low contrast** (charcoal with black; tonal beige; shades of blue): calm, soft, "quiet luxury", and lets the outline and texture show.
The web consensus: high contrast reads more formal and authoritative, low contrast softer and more approachable. One useful note: value and saturation are separate, so a muted outfit can still be high contrast.

**How to see it in a photo.** Squint, or imagine the photo in black and white. Rank top, outer layer, trousers and shoes from lightest to darkest. Big jumps = high contrast; one or two steps = medium; all within one step = low. Then look at *where* the jumps are: at the waistband (makes the waist break, 2), at the feet (pulls the eye down), near the collar (frames the top).

**Where it bends.**
- Low contrast fails when it is *accidental and flat*: all-black in one identical black looks like a uniform. FA calls it the restaurant-host look and fixes it with different blacks and faded pieces (Vn6KlXkU2xA [02:08]–[03:11]). Low contrast works when the depth or texture varies (Derek Guy, Feb 2025: a tonal outfit still has "contrast through texture, sheen, and shade").
- High contrast at the feet (white sneakers under black trousers) is a classic, but TD avoids it when he wants the eye up (wIib9hzVUuw [08:07]).
- Layer order: TD's rule of thumb is lighter inside, darker outside, "not the case 100% of the time" (wIib9hzVUuw [08:38]).

**How it interacts.** With 3 (dark = heavy), 2 (the waist line needs a lightness gap), 10 (tonal dressing = low hue contrast, deliberate value steps), 11 (TD and WK count shades of one colour as half a colour because the contrast between them is weaker), 12 (an accent pops by value as much as by hue), 14 (texture replaces value contrast in tonal outfits).

**What the four say.**
- TD: "lighter colors closer to the body and your darker colors further away" (wIib9hzVUuw [08:38]); shades of one colour count as half because "the contrast between them will not be as visually striking" (IUMqpLeCT-E [02:11]); two denims look better in "slightly different washes" (wIib9hzVUuw [04:35]); darker colours for evenings (vQ6_okzfNSs [03:12]); break up black outfits "with some lighter colors like white" (RrGbfY6NTZI [05:55]).
- WK: dark outfits need a light touch, bright ones need dark: "the darker color is balanced by the light color" (rNCrnP-os0U [05:39]); shades of blue "is the easiest way to dress" (uBsIrt-zU0I [03:57]), navy top with lighter jeans or the reverse.
- FA: in all-black, "the lack of depth and movement" is the failure (Vn6KlXkU2xA [02:08]); vary the blacks (vintage wash, dark grey, fading).
- JA: does not discuss lightness as such; his base palette is "blue and black and white" (Pxeeh-caTJg [22:43]).
- **Agreement:** TD, WK and FA all want some value variation, even inside one colour. None of them uses the words "value contrast"; they say "shades", "tones", "washes", "lighter/darker".

**Can the AI see it today?** No direct field. Two options: (a) estimate value from `color_family` with a fixed lookup (black 1, charcoal 2, navy 2, denim_dark 3, olive 3, brown 3, grey 4, denim_mid 4, sage 5, denim_light 6, tan 6, cream 8, white 9…), cheap but coarse (a "grey" can be light or dark); or (b) add a `value` field (light / mid / dark) to the reading. (b) is better for 3, 4, 6, 10 and 12 at once.

## 7. Saturation (muted vs bright) and why muted reads premium

**In plain words.** Saturation is how pure or intense a colour is. Muted colours are greyed, dusty or deepened (olive, burgundy, rust, sage, faded navy); bright colours are pure (fire-engine red, kelly green, cobalt, neon). The consensus among the four and the web: muted, deeper versions of a colour read more expensive and grown-up; bright versions read playful, youthful or loud.

Why it reads premium (no research found; the stylist explanation): muted colours suggest natural dyes and natural fibres (wool, linen, suede) and age well; uniform bright colour reads mass-produced, sporty or promotional. Treat this as a cultural signal, not a law.

**How to see it in a photo.** For each coloured piece: would you call it "dusty / deep / earthy" or "pure / loud / neon"? Count the bright pieces and note their size and position. One small bright piece is an accent (12); a bright piece the size of a jacket or trousers sets the whole tone.

**Where it bends.**
- A bright piece can be the deliberate point: WK and FA both like one bright item in a dark outfit (12).
- Some styles live on brights: Y2K, colourful streetwear, summer resort wear. JA calls Y2K "one of the most loudest" styles and "not beginner friendly" (5Dfr0PXTTpM [03:34]), not wrong.
- Bright is not the problem; *many* brights are. WK: all bright "just looks too happy and light" (rNCrnP-os0U [05:07]).
- A bright piece surrounded by dark muted pieces reads controlled (WK tNVdA_arEa8 [00:31]).

**How it interacts.** With 12 (the accent is usually the one bright thing), 9 (complementary pairs work in menswear mostly *because* they are muted: navy and brown), 6 (bright plus high value contrast is the loudest combination), 11 (several brights quickly break the three-colour limit), 3 (bright = heavy).

**What the four say.**

## 8. Colour temperature (warm vs cool, earth tones)

**In plain words.** Warm colours lean toward red, orange and yellow (brown, tan, camel, rust, mustard, olive, cream, burgundy); cool colours lean toward blue and green-blue (navy, grey, charcoal, blue, icy white). Earth tones are the warm muted family (olive, forest, brown, tan, rust, cream). Black, white and denim sit in the middle and work with both. The web's usable point: keep the outfit's temperature mostly consistent, or mix on purpose with one warm against one cool (navy and brown is the textbook mix). Most web temperature advice is about the wearer's skin undertone; that part is dropped here.

**How to see it in a photo.** Sort each piece into warm, cool or neutral (black, white, mid denim, true grey). All warm (earth tones), all cool (blue-grey), or a clear warm-versus-cool pair are all fine. The thing to flag is a scatter: three warm and three cool pieces with no lead.

**Where it bends.**
- Warm against cool is often the best pairing: navy with brown (TD), blue denim with a tan or rust piece.
- A single warm accent in a cool outfit works as the point: TD's burgundy in cooler outfits (CJv4TGLrdig [04:06]).
- Black and white are neutral and mix with anything (WK ew3gCk1zkso [04:19]).
- One known clash from menswear guides: black shoes with a brown suit, read as a temperature mismatch. In casual clothes it matters much less.

**How it interacts.** With 9 (complementary pairs are usually a warm and a cool colour), 7 (temperature only reads clearly in muted, mid-value colours; in neon it is drowned out), 11 (a coherent warm palette can hold more colours than a scattered one), 14 (earth tones and textured fabrics go together in fall and winter).

**What the four say.**
- TD: "burgundy is a great little accent piece in cooler tone outfits" (CJv4TGLrdig [04:06]); dark brown for those who wear earth tones; blue and brown work as opposites, and green with brown "leaning more towards that earthy color palette" (RrGbfY6NTZI [07:01]); suggests dark brown jeans for earth-tone fans (vQ6_okzfNSs [01:05]).
- JA: earth tones as a style of their own: "Earth tones is more about like very soft colors." olive, forest green, browns (5Dfr0PXTTpM [02:34]).
- FA: builds his palette from places and memories, "earthy you know washed out colors" (TQi2YaZ5yNY [04:42]), rather than warm-versus-cool rules.
- WK: no temperature talk found; his tools are shades, toning down and one point colour.
- **Agreement:** no one teaches warm vs cool as a rule. TD and JA treat earth tones as a coherent family; TD likes warm-cool pairs. Low priority as a scoring rule.

**Can the AI see it today?** Not as a field; derivable from `color_family` with a fixed warm / cool / neutral table. Cheap to add, low risk. Do not use it as a fault on its own; use it inside 9 and 11.

## 9. Complementary, analogous and triadic pairings in menswear

**In plain words.**
- **Complementary:** opposite on the colour wheel (blue and orange, red and green, purple and yellow). Strong contrast.
- **Analogous:** neighbours on the wheel (blue and green, olive and brown, rust and tan). Calm, cohesive.
- **Triadic:** three evenly spaced (red, yellow, blue). Loud, hard to wear.
What actually works in menswear: complementary pairs **in muted, deepened form**: navy with brown (brown is a dark muted orange), olive with burgundy, navy with rust or tan. Analogous earth-tone palettes. Triadic almost never, outside of deliberate colourful styles. The wheel is a starting point; saturation (7) and value (6) decide whether a pair looks good.

**How to see it in a photo.** First take out the neutrals (black, white, grey, denim, often navy, beige). Then look at what is left. Two colours opposite each other: complementary; is at least one muted and is one clearly the lead? Two neighbours: analogous. Three spread out and all bright: triadic, usually a fault outside its style.

**Where it bends.**
- A "clash" pair can work. The founder's example: a red shirt over somewhat baggy blue trousers, with good proportions, "might be a 7.8… or an 8". Red against blue is near-complementary; it works when one is muted or darker, the shapes are right, and nothing else competes.
- Pure complementary brights (kelly green and red) read like a costume or a holiday.
- TD: the common rule "never wear navy and black together" is wrong: "those two colors go beautifully together" (vQd36IKIkOg [06:06]). Wheel-based rules have exceptions.
- FA shows wheel-correct outfits that look bad and wheel-wrong outfits that look good, and works from a personal palette instead (TQi2YaZ5yNY [03:09], [11:05]).

**How it interacts.** With 7 (muted makes complementary wearable), 6 (a value gap between the pair helps one lead), 11 (a complementary accent is the "10%"), 12 (the accent is often the complement of the main colour: an orange sock with navy), 5 (good proportions earn a riskier pair; bad proportions make it look worse).

**What the four say.**
- FA, first tip of a 2023 video: "complementary colors are contrasting colors on the color spectrum" and naturally satisfying to the eye (fZ2Jc3gOZMI [00:00]); "visually we find contrast very satisfying" (TnNv7Zvui6E [04:05]); later (2024) moves past the wheel: "think of my wardrobe through the lens of a color palette" (TQi2YaZ5yNY [03:41]), "you're using colors because they work for you" (TQi2YaZ5yNY [11:05]), and avoid "matchy matchy" (TQi2YaZ5yNY [04:11]).
- TD: he once followed the wheel (brown with blue, analogous blue-green, triadic red-yellow-blue) and learned "the color wheel works great in theory" but not always in practice; "Neutrals play a crucial role to anchor a look." (wIib9hzVUuw [02:02]–[02:32]); "blue naturally looks good with brown" (RrGbfY6NTZI [07:01]).
- WK: warns against pairing levels that don't match: a "really saturated blue" with a "really pale pink" (ew3gCk1zkso [09:39]): a saturation and value point, not a hue point.
- JA: "matching materials, matching colors subtly" (ctdMGExbNws [08:41]); no wheel talk.
- **Agreement:** all four say the wheel is not enough. TD and FA both learned it and then moved to "neutrals anchor, one palette". Navy (or blue) with brown is the one pairing praised by name.

**Can the AI see it today?** Hue can be derived from `color_family`. A rule should never fire on hue alone; it needs `saturation` and value (6) too, and which piece is the lead (by size). Triadic-and-bright is the only safe fault to detect.

## 10. Tonal (one hue, many depths) vs tone-on-tone (many hues, one depth) vs monochrome

**In plain words, and a naming warning.** Three different things:
- **One hue, several depths:** navy top, mid-blue jeans, light-blue shirt; or black, charcoal, grey. The English word for this is "tonal" dressing.
- **Several hues at one depth and saturation:** dusty sage, dusty blue and stone, all the same soft level. Harmonious because nothing jumps in lightness.
- **Monochrome:** one colour head to toe at about one depth (all black, all cream).

**The names are reversed between sources.** In Korean and Japanese usage, 톤온톤 / tone-on-tone = same hue, different brightness, and 톤인톤 / tone-in-tone = same tone, different hue (hikoco 2021; FASHIONSNAP dictionary). WK's 2024 video gives the opposite labels (e2MgmGDLV9Q [00:00]–[01:04]), and in 2023 he admitted "I don't actually know which one's which" (ew3gCk1zkso [08:35]). The founder's brief follows WK's 2024 labels. **Recommendation:** in the rulebook and in Kit's words, name them by what they do ("shades of one colour", "same depth, different colours", "one colour head to toe") and never by the Korean labels.

**How to see it in a photo.** Remove the accent. Is everything one hue family at different depths (one-hue tonal)? Different hues but no piece much lighter or brighter than the rest (same depth)? One colour at one depth (monochrome)? Then check what keeps it from going flat: depth steps, texture or sheen, a single accent.

**Where it bends.**
- Monochrome fails when it is one flat shade with one texture (FA's restaurant-host black). It works with mixed blacks or greys and mixed fabrics (FA, TD's gray-on-gray, JA's "Different materials on every black").
- Same-depth mixes are hard: WK says if you're just off a little, a pale piece next to a strong one "can kind of just look really unbalanced" (e2MgmGDLV9Q [01:34]).
- FA dislikes the over-matched version: shoes, jacket and everything in the shoes' colour, which "put yourself in a box" (TQi2YaZ5yNY [10:04]–[10:34]). Monochrome centred on one bold colour is different, and he allows it.
- TD on height-based monochrome advice: he rejects the "if you're short, wear monochrome" rule as over-prescribed (vQd36IKIkOg [00:36]). For the app, monochrome is a style choice, never a body fix.

**How it interacts.** With 6 (tonal = low hue contrast with deliberate value steps), 14 (texture is what makes tonal look rich), 12 (a monochrome base is the best stage for one accent), 11 (shades of one colour count as half or one colour), 5 (with no colour contrast, the outline is all you see).

**What the four say.**
- WK, the most: the two Korean concepts (e2MgmGDLV9Q [00:00]–[01:34]): one hue "when you match the same color but in different fortitude" ("fortitude", caption unclear, probably "brightness"); "tone on tone is when you match different colors of the same tone". Shades of blue and shades of black are his "easiest way to dress" (uBsIrt-zU0I [03:57]–[05:06]).
- TD: "I love a monochrome outfit" with gray as one of the best colours for it (XIYvycsZxDk [00:33]); gray on gray "Especially when you incorporate different textures" (-e9Is50-fn0 [02:44]); a tonal gray outfit with gray baggy jeans (vQ6_okzfNSs [01:38]); all black is "foolproof" (-e9Is50-fn0 [08:46]).
- JA: monochrome as his own style: "Different materials on every black, but always wearing black." (Ulx1vjAREu0 [13:49]); "Monochrome is a great style to get into if you don't like color" (Ulx1vjAREu0 [14:19]).
- FA: all-black is the best palette but needs tones and textures (Vn6KlXkU2xA [00:00]–[07:35]); shades and textures "stack up to create interesting looks" (Jh8dz-KZcfY [04:50]).
- **Agreement:** all four like tonal and monochrome dressing **with depth or texture variation**. Only FA warns against over-matching colour across pieces.

**Can the AI see it today?** Partly. `color_family` has depth pairs (denim_light / mid / dark; black / charcoal / grey; cream / white), so "one hue, several depths" is detectable. Same-depth mixes need the value read (6). The rescue factor (texture) is not read.

## 11. The 60-30-10 rule and "max three colours"

**In plain words.**
- **Max three colours** (the creators' version): no more than three colours in an outfit. Black and white count as half a colour each; several shades of one colour count as one, or half each.
- **60-30-10** (an interior-design rule borrowed by style blogs): about 60% one main colour (the biggest piece: trousers, coat or suit), 30% a second (shirt, knit), 10% an accent (shoes, cap, bag, socks, scarf). None of the four uses it; it shows up on the web only, as a rough guide.

**How to see it in a photo.** List every piece's colour, including shoes, cap and bag. Group shades of one colour together. Count: black or white = 0.5, every other colour = 1. Up to 2.5 is safe; 3 is fine if it's deliberate; over 3 is the "disjointed" risk. For 60-30-10, check whether one colour clearly leads by area and the smallest one sits on a small item.

**Where it bends.**
- Both TD and WK call it a beginner's guide: TD says colourful outfits "can sometimes work" but are hard to style (IUMqpLeCT-E [01:40]); WK says "unless you know what you're doing" (ew3gCk1zkso [04:49]).
- Neutral-heavy outfits can hold more pieces without looking busy (TD: any two or three neutrals work together, 0JtnbgtbQfE [04:20]).
- Pattern counts: a multicolour pattern can add two or three colours by itself (TD's multicolour scarf, wIib9hzVUuw [06:07]).
- Socks are an accent slot the app cannot see.

**How it interacts.** With 10 (shades count as half: tonal outfits pass easily), 12 (the 10% is the accent), 7 (several brights break the count fastest; several muted colours read fewer), 8 (a coherent earth-tone palette can hold more), 9 (a complementary accent is the classic 10%).

**What the four say.**
- TD: "you shall not use colors in excess of three" (IUMqpLeCT-E [01:40]); shades of one colour can "count each of those Shades as half colors" (IUMqpLeCT-E [02:11]).
- WK, most detailed: "black and white are half colors everything else is one color" (e2MgmGDLV9Q [06:15]); "as a fashion newbie you should never dress more than three colors" (e2MgmGDLV9Q [06:15]); "people that wear Too Many Colors they just look disjointed" (e2MgmGDLV9Q [06:45]); counts famous outfits at 1.5–2.5 colours (ew3gCk1zkso [04:49]–[05:54]).
- JA: no count; neutrals base "blue and black and white", then "you add the things that pop" (Pxeeh-caTJg [22:43]).
- FA: no count; a personal palette instead (TQi2YaZ5yNY).
- **Agreement:** TD and WK give the identical rule (three max, black and white as half, shades as half). JA and FA don't count but both keep a neutral base. 60-30-10 is not from the four; don't put it in the rulebook as theirs.

**Can the AI see it today?** Yes for the count: group `color_family` values by hue, weight black and white as 0.5. Socks are not read. Patterned pieces: `pattern` is read, but the colours inside a pattern are not, so a multicolour pattern is under-counted.

## 12. Accent or "pop" colour, and where it sits

**In plain words.** One small element that breaks an otherwise calm palette and gives the eye a place to land: an orange sock with all black, a red bag, a cream cap that picks up a colour elsewhere. WK uses the Korean word **"point"** (포인트). Rules of thumb: one accent (two at most), small in area, muted or bright but clearly different, and placed where you want the eye. Echoing it once elsewhere (WK's "threading") ties it in, but obvious matching looks try-hard.

**How to see it in a photo.** Find the one piece that differs most from the rest in hue, brightness or material. Is it small (shoes, cap, bag, socks, scarf, an inner layer at the collar)? Is it the only one? Where is it: near the collar (pulls the eye up, the safest spot), at the waist, or at the feet (pulls the eye down)? Is the same colour repeated quietly somewhere else?

**Where it bends.**
- The accent can be a texture, not a colour: WK: a ribbed or waffle texture, or "something nylon" in an all-cotton outfit, can be the point (1wUp3RSAEqI [01:01]–[01:31]).
- The accent can be a formality break: sporty shoes with semi-formal clothes (WK e2MgmGDLV9Q [02:05]); TD's statement colour in monochrome (IUMqpLeCT-E [01:09]).
- At the feet: TD avoids a contrasting shoe when he wants the gaze up (wIib9hzVUuw [08:07]); FA and WK like colour at the feet (socks). Both are fine; they make different focal points.
- More than one or two accents is clutter: JA's "One or two details will always hit stronger" than four or five (RIuzSb8OLMM [10:41]).
- Threading done loudly ("here's a green here's a green", WK rNCrnP-os0U [06:41]) or FA's shoe-matched jacket (TQi2YaZ5yNY [10:34]) is a fault: matchy.

**How it interacts.** With 10 (a monochrome or tonal base makes any accent read), 11 (it is the 10%; it costs one colour of the three), 7 (one bright piece is an accent; three are a loud outfit), 6 (the accent pops by value too: white socks in all black), 3 (the accent is a deliberate heavy spot; place it to balance the outfit).

**What the four say.**
- WK: "the point is basically an accent a little cherry on top" (e2MgmGDLV9Q [01:34]), with all black and orange socks as his example; "Everything can become an accent with the right context." (1wUp3RSAEqI [01:31]); a dark outfit needs "a little light touch of color" such as a white tee, white socks or brighter sneakers (rNCrnP-os0U [05:39]); threading "the subtler you do it the better" (rNCrnP-os0U [06:41]).
- FA: a red bag on an all-black fit "almost glues everything together with a nice focal point" (Jh8dz-KZcfY [03:47]); little sock pops; small moments that are "never the focal point" but add up (Jh8dz-KZcfY [07:01]).
- TD: a cream cap gives "a little pop of color that ties back in" with the jeans (wIib9hzVUuw [05:36]); "I don't really want the eye to be drawn towards the feet" (wIib9hzVUuw [08:07]); "I love a pop of color as much as the next guy" but neutrals win for speed (0JtnbgtbQfE [04:53]); burgundy as an accent in cool outfits (CJv4TGLrdig [04:06]).

## 13. Trouser break and hem against the shoe

**In plain words.** The break is the fold where the trouser hem meets the shoe.
- **No break:** the hem just reaches the top of the shoe, no fold. Slim and tapered trousers, loafers, sleek sneakers.
- **Slight (quarter) break:** the hem touches the shoe with one small fold. The modern default for straight trousers, jeans, sneakers.
- **Full / medium break:** a clear single fold resting on the shoe. Fuller tailored trousers, dress shoes.
- **Stacked:** several folds gathered over the shoe. Streetwear and wide jeans over sneakers or boots; sloppy on tailored trousers.
- **Cropped:** ends above the ankle, shows sock or ankle. Slim or straight trousers, loafers; ends clearly above the ankle, not just short of the shoe.
- **Pooling:** too long, fabric dragging on or past the heel.
Leg width changes the right answer: a wide leg needs more length (slight to full break, or a deliberate stack) or it flaps short; a slim leg wants less (no break or slight) or it bunches. The NYT (Sep 2026) describes the current mood as a middle ground, "some break but not enough to appear sloppy".

**How to see it in a photo.** Look at the bottom 15 centimetres of the trouser against the shoe. How many folds? Does the hem cover the top of the shoe, touch it, or end above it? Is there a gap of sock (cropped) and does it look deliberate? Then check against the leg width: slim + many folds = too long; wide + ends above the shoe = too short unless it's a deliberate wide crop; wide + soft gathers over a chunky sneaker = deliberate stack.

**Where it bends.**
- Stacking is style-dependent. TD calls more than one break "stacking territory" and sloppier and more casual (SNjfZ5NKndc [00:30]), yet praises massive cargos "nicely stacking on top of these Salamon sneakers" (XIYvycsZxDk [01:07]). FA wants "a nice organic looking cozy little bit of stack" over sneakers, not the 2016 skinny stack (8rmkOpCbFTM [06:37]).
- Boots: FA likes some space so the boots show, but trousers over the boot is "not a bad look" either (TnNv7Zvui6E [10:45]).
- Winter: TD's "cardinal sin" is bare ankles in winter with no-show socks (IUMqpLeCT-E [03:47]); a crop is a warm-weather choice.
- Flares and bootcuts need length to read (JA's flares sit on the shoe).
- Sweatpants and cuffed (elastic) hems are their own case: gathered at the ankle on purpose (FA 8rmkOpCbFTM [09:22]; JA tucking sweats into socks, Pxeeh-caTJg [02:02]).

**How it interacts.** With 4 and 5 (the hem finishes the outline; the shoe must match the leg volume: FA's slim shoes with a long or medium inseam, bulky shoes with bigger trousers, 8rmkOpCbFTM [10:27]–[11:02]), 1 (a pooled hem shortens the long block), 6 and 12 (trousers matching the shoe colour keep the eye up, TD), 14 (season: crops and bare ankles in summer, length and socks in winter).

**What the four say.**
- TD: "Pant break will quite literally make or break your outfit." (SNjfZ5NKndc [00:00]); the perfect break should "touch the shoe" with "one clean break" (SNjfZ5NKndc [00:00]); "Anything more than one break goes into stacking territory" (SNjfZ5NKndc [00:30]); his fixed outfit: "straight cut, one break on the shoe, just perfect" (wIib9hzVUuw [10:40]); he admires trousers "barely breaking on the shoes" (0ilmLux6mPg [08:15]).
- FA: the pants-shoes relationship is "the silent killer of most people's outfits" (XtcY-zmoS-E [05:08]); "90% of the time it is a length problem" (XtcY-zmoS-E [05:38]), so hem them; for sneakers "it looks really good when Pants fall on the shoe" (TnNv7Zvui6E [10:15]); "you don't need as much crop as you think" (TnNv7Zvui6E [10:15]); "a nice generous pair of pants stacking over top" chunky shoes (8rmkOpCbFTM [04:58]).
- JA: lists "stacking pants" as something beginners copy without understanding (UcLBvcmnnus [02:10]); wears flares long over boots.
- WK: no break talk found in these transcripts.
- **Agreement:** TD and FA agree the hem is one of the biggest factors and that a single soft break on the shoe is the safe default. They split on stacking only by style: both accept a soft stack on wide trousers over chunky sneakers; both reject the skinny stack and pooling on tailored trousers.

**Can the AI see it today?** Weak. `hem` has the right values (pooling / full_break / slight_break / no_break / cuffed / cropped) but is a known weak read, and `leg_shape` slim vs straight is also weak, so "too long for this leg" is hard to judge. There is no value for a deliberate stack separate from pooling. Socks (the winter rule) are not read. ## 14. Texture contrast and layering depth, by season

**In plain words.** Texture is the surface of the fabric: smooth cotton, ribbed or chunky knit, brushed flannel, corduroy, suede, leather, denim, wool, nylon, linen. **Texture contrast** = putting different surfaces next to each other (a knit under a leather jacket, wool trousers with suede shoes) so the outfit has depth even without colour. **Layering depth** = the number of visible layers and how they stack: thin inside, heavier outside; two or three layers; each layer showing a little (a collar, a hem, a hood).
By season: in fall and winter, texture and layers do most of the work (wool, cashmere, corduroy, suede, chunky knits). In summer there are no layers, so the trouser shape, one good tee, the tuck, a light overshirt or a texture like linen or a waffle knit carry the outfit.

**How to see it in a photo.** Count visible layers and check each one shows (inner collar, hem below the jacket, hood). Check the order: does each layer look thicker or heavier than the one under it? Name the surfaces: are at least two clearly different (matte against shiny, smooth against nubby)? Two of the same chunky texture stacked (knit over knit) is the common miss. Then check against the season: a heavy layered outfit in a heat photo, or bare ankles and a tee in snow.

**Where it bends.**
- Same-texture layering can work when the weights differ clearly; TD's specific miss was a knitted cardigan on a knitted sweater (wIib9hzVUuw [05:06]).
- The thin-to-thick rule "doesn't work 100% of the time" (TD, IUMqpLeCT-E [03:47]); a light shirt-jacket over a chunky knit is a known style.
- More is not better: JA caps it at "two to three layers" (RIuzSb8OLMM [10:11]); WK's "point" can be a single texture in an otherwise plain outfit.
- In all-black and tonal outfits, texture stops being optional and becomes the main thing (FA, TD, JA, Derek Guy).

**How it interacts.** With 10 and 6 (texture replaces colour and value contrast in tonal outfits), 3 (chunky texture = heavy; it shifts balance up), 4 (each top layer adds top volume over the trousers), 2 (a cropped outer layer over a longer base can make the waist line), 12 (one texture can be the accent), 13 (season ties hem and layering together).

**What the four say.**
- TD: "you shall layer from thin to thick" (IUMqpLeCT-E [03:15]); "the right texture will add depth to your outfit" without relying on colour or silhouette, "Especially in the colder temperatures" (SNjfZ5NKndc [03:32]); "it would have been better to contrast the textures" (wIib9hzVUuw [05:06]); "this outfit is all about texture" (XIYvycsZxDk [00:33]).
- FA: "the dimension the contrast the composition it's all in the layering" (Jh8dz-KZcfY [00:00]); in all-black, use "the way light interacts with your clothes" (Vn6KlXkU2xA [05:56]), leather boots with denim being the easiest; "fading and texture fuses the piece just a bit" so it pairs more easily (TnNv7Zvui6E [11:16]); in summer heat he wears just a tight tee or tank with wide trousers (XtcY-zmoS-E [03:37]).
- JA: base layer fits close; "two to three layers is enough" (RIuzSb8OLMM [10:11]); the second layer is "where structure and contrast come in" (RIuzSb8OLMM [09:41]); most people "stack pieces without purpose" (Jz9QVWgBbOg [21:49]); fabric and texture "matters drastically" (CI-wkEOHRAg [03:36]).
- WK: "focus on shape and texture more than color and Logo" (dKl6pohkzoA [02:34]); a texture change (cotton to nylon) can be the point (1wUp3RSAEqI [01:31]).
- **Agreement:** all four; texture is the one thing every creator calls underrated. TD and JA agree on thin-to-thick and 2–3 layers.

**Can the AI see it today?** No. Fabric and texture are not read at all. `shows_below_outer` gives whether an inner layer peeks out, and the garment count gives the number of layers, but not whether they contrast or get heavier outward. Weather (approximate location, never stored) gives the season. **This is the largest blind spot**: the concept all four call underrated, and the one that rescues tonal and all-black outfits, is invisible to the reading. A `surface` field (smooth / knit / brushed or napped / leather or shiny / denim / technical) would unlock 10, 12 and 14 at once.

## How the concepts lean on each other (the short version)

- **Proportion cluster (1, 2, 4, 5, 13):** one question seen five ways: where the outfit narrows and ends. The waist break makes the thirds; the thirds and the top/bottom widths make the silhouette; the hem finishes it. Score them together, not five times.
- **Weight cluster (3, 6, 7, 12):** darkness, brightness and size decide where the eye goes. An accent is a small, deliberate heavy spot; a top-heavy outfit is a badly placed heavy spot.
- **Palette cluster (7, 8, 9, 10, 11):** saturation and value decide whether a hue pairing works; the wheel alone does not. Muted + one lead colour + at most three colours (black and white as half) covers most of what the four say.
- **Rescue pairs** (the nuance the founder cares about): good proportions earn a riskier colour pair (9); texture rescues low contrast (14 → 6, 10); one accent rescues a flat neutral outfit (12 → 10); a matching shoe colour keeps a high-contrast outfit pointed upward (6 → 12); a chunky shoe balances a big top (3 → 4).
- **Where the four disagree:** wide-top, slim-bottom (FA says avoid; TD and JA say it's a choice); how much crop (FA and JA like it; WK and TD prefer an at-belt hem or tuck); stacking (TD's sloppy unless wide and casual; FA likes a soft stack); colour-wheel rules (FA, 2023, for; FA 2024 and TD, against as a rule).

## What the reading would need to judge these properly

Ranked by how many concepts each unlocks:
1. **Value** (light / mid / dark per garment): 3, 4, 6, 10, 12. A `color_family` lookup is a $0 stopgap.
2. **Surface / texture** (smooth, knit, brushed, leather or shiny, denim, technical): 10, 12, 14.
3. **Better `hem` and slim-vs-straight reads**: 4, 5, 13.
4. **Trouser rise** (low / mid / high): 1, 2, 5.
5. **Socks** (visible, colour): 11, 12, 13 (winter).
