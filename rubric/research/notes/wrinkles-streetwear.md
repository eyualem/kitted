# Wrinkles on casual and streetwear clothes (Oct 14, 2026)

**The question (founder, Oct 14):** today [CON-1] takes −0.4 off for wrinkles on any piece the AI is clearly sure about (−0.2 on drawstring trousers and camp shirts). He thinks cotton tees and other streetwear pieces that aren't fully ironed shouldn't count against you. Is he right, and where should the line be?

**Short answer:** mostly yes. Soft wrinkles on knit tops (tees, hoodies, sweatshirts) and on casual woven pieces worn in a casual look are accepted, and some people like them. A crumpled dress shirt, chinos or tailoring still reads as sloppy, and so does anything wrinkled at an interview. The AI can tell which piece it is and the code knows the look, so the main change can be built today. It can't tell soft rumpling from fresh fold creases or a crumpled collar.

**What this adds up to:** no creator says a soft, lived-in tee or hoodie is a fault. One (Wanhee, said strongly and repeatedly) prefers it. The one look all of them dislike is **new and stiff with fold creases** (Wanhee), and that is the opposite of what [CON-1] charges for.

## 2. What the web says (2023–2026 preferred; dates noted)

**Knit tops (tees, heavyweight and boxy tees): nobody expects them pressed.**
- Asphalte, a heavyweight-tee brand (care guide, 2026 site): "This jersey barely creases"; the tee "isn't intended to look ironed". Steam lightly at most.
- Acaio (boxy tee listing) says not to iron at all. Weekday, Printify and Bonfire say never to iron over a print.
- Buck Mason (boxy heavy tee listing): a heavier boxy tee "breaks in over time".
- Gear Patrol (Jan 2023), on vintage tees: a good one is "very soft and worn in but with the seams intact". The softened fabric and faded dye (the patina) are part of what people pay for. Highsnobiety ties the streetwear vintage-tee trend to Jerry Lorenzo.
- An anti-wrinkle view: Care of Carl (Aug 2024) says "wrinkles and pilling have no place on clothing", but its example is shirts and its fix for knitwear is a fabric shaver, so it means pilling there, not soft wrinkles.

**Casual woven shirts (oxford, flannel, overshirt, linen): some rumpling is fine in a casual look.**
- Art of Manliness, oxford guide (Oct 2023, updated Nov 2025): "a little rumpledness is part of its DNA"; for a relaxed look, "don't iron it at all."
- Primer, linen guide (Mar 2026): creases where the body bends are "natural", but avoid "huge, all over wrinkles" from neglect. The one place to curb wrinkles is "your work wardrobe."
- He Spoke Style (2017, older): linen "without wrinkles can just look stiff," but leave the house looking freshly pressed and let the day add the creases.
- Wallpaper* / MR Magazine (Apr 2025): S/S 2025 menswear (Prada) showed clothes "creased and crumpled", as if they had lived a life. 10 Magazine (2019, older) says the same about Martine Rose and others: crumpling done on purpose, with control.

**Dress shirts, trousers and tailoring: pressed is still expected.**
- Resident (Mar 2025): "Nothing destroys a sharp appearance faster than a wrinkled shirt."
- Care of Carl (Aug 2024): "Even an expensive shirt will appear less extravagant if it's wrinkled."
- Maurilio Amorim (2011, older but the clearest ranking): shirts under a suit must be ironed; striped and solid shirts mostly pressed; casual plaids optional; shirts "made to be wrinkled" look best that way.
- For interviews, career guides and an employer survey (undated, a Fish4Jobs study) list a badly wrinkled shirt among the mistakes; 19% of HR managers lose interest over a sloppy appearance.

**Lived-in or sloppy? Where the sources draw the line:**
1. **Type of fabric.** Knit jersey and fleece, and soft-washed or garment-dyed cotton, are made to look lived-in. Crisp poplin and dress shirting are made to be pressed.
2. **Type of wrinkle.** Creases where you bend (elbows, waist, the back of the knee) are normal. Big creases all over the piece, or a sharp line from the dryer or the packet, are neglect (Primer). Wanhee singles out the packet fold.
3. **Where it shows.** A rumpled body on a casual shirt is fine. A collapsed collar or wavy placket on a collared shirt reads undone (a common tip is to press only the collar, cuffs and placket when short on time). No dated source measured this.
4. **The look and the occasion.** Wrinkles fit streetwear, workwear, linen in summer and relaxed weekend looks. They don't fit tailoring, work clothes or interviews.
5. **On purpose vs neglect.** Designer crumpling and worn-in vintage look chosen. The same wrinkles on an outfit with no clear idea read as not caring.

**A caution from our own gold set:** the AI judging panel (#224) did sometimes dock a wrinkled tee, but almost always on outfits that were weak anyway: a teal tee with plaid shorts and sport sandals ("reads sloppy", G015/G173) and a long grey tee with no shape (G092). On the streetwear photos it docked wrinkled *shorts and pants* (G011, G080), not tees. Its strongest wrinkle complaints were about woven shirts and chinos (G062, G101, G108, G119, G149). The panel is all AI, so this is weak evidence, but it points to the same line.

## 3. The recommended rule

**Stop charging for wrinkles on:**
- **Knit tops on any look:** tee, graphic tee, long-sleeve tee, hoodie, sweatshirt, tank top. The jersey barely creases, so a "wrinkled" read here is most often soft drape or folds on a boxy cut. Worn-in is a plus for Wanhee and accepted everywhere else. One exception: a tee worn under a blazer, sport coat or suit jacket keeps the half charge (−0.2), because there it is part of a tailored outfit.
- **Casual woven pieces, when the outfit reads as streetwear, workwear, vintage sport or outdoor:** overshirt, flannel, chore coat and field jacket, camp shirt, denim, cargos, shorts, drawstring trousers. The founder's call fits these looks: a lived-in overshirt with wide jeans and boots is part of the style.

**Charge half (−0.2):**
- The same casual woven pieces in any other look (minimal, prep, smart casual, no clear style).
- **An oxford or button-down shirt with jeans on an everyday day.** A rumpled oxford is classic, but it is the piece people say needs a quick press.
- Linen-like pieces stay at −0.2 as now.

**Keep −0.4:**
- Dress shirts, trousers, suit trousers, chinos, blazers, sport coats and suit jackets on every look.
- **Any woven shirt or pants at an Interview or Event, whatever the look.**

**Fresh fold creases (sharp packet lines):** don't charge for them today. The creators dislike them, but the AI reads them as plain "wrinkled", so they can't be told apart from soft wear. On a dress shirt they are already charged −0.4 under the rule above. On a tee the cost of missing them is small. If it matters later, a narrow second-look question ("sharp fold lines from packaging: yes/no", about $0.30 to test) could turn them into a no-points tip ("Wash it once to soften the fold lines").

**A crumpled dress shirt:** still costs −0.4, and a collapsed collar should too. Today it only counts if the AI calls the whole shirt wrinkled.

**What stays the same:** [CON-5] (only reads the AI is clear about count), [CON-2] (no check in dim light), stains and pilling (−1.2 / −0.4), and stretched necklines staying under "worn". A wrinkle never blocks a credit (ruling 46). Where a charge is waived, show nothing: not even a tip, because it isn't a fault.

## 4. What the AI reading can tell apart today

The reading gives one `condition` per piece (`clean | wrinkled | pilled_or_worn | stained`) plus `certainty`, alongside `subcategory`, `distressed_by_design` and `strain`.

| Detail the rule needs | Readable today? |
|---|---|
| Which piece (tee vs oxford vs dress shirt vs chinos) | **Yes**, from `subcategory` |
| Which look the outfit reads as | **Yes**, the code decides it (`detectStyles`) |
| Occasion; worn under a blazer | **Yes** |
| Soft rumple vs big creases all over | **No**: one "wrinkled" value covers "visible creases or rumpling" |
| Fresh packet fold lines | **No** (would need a new field or a second-look question) |
| Wrinkled collar or placket only | **No** (v4's `collar_collapsed` field was tried and the v4 format failed the steadiness test, #63) |
| Linen vs cotton | **No**; drawstring trousers and camp shirts stand in for linen |
| Tight-fit "creases" (Tim's complaint) | **Partly**: that is `strain`, which Fit already handles |

So the garment and look rules in §3 can be built today in code, with no new reading and nothing extra to pay. The fold-crease and collar ideas would each need a new reading, which needs the founder's OK and a steadiness test.

**How often it applies:** across the gold set's 1,456 garments (not counting shoes), 65 read "wrinkled": dress shirts 10, oxfords 9, shorts 8, tees 7 (+2 graphic tees), chinos 7, trousers 5, camp shirts 5, flannels and cargos 2 each. On the founder's own repeat-read photos (`zoom1`), overshirts lead (13 reads), then drawstring trousers (8). No hoodie or sweatshirt read "wrinkled" anywhere. Under the rule, roughly a third of today's wrinkle charges in the gold set go away or halve. The founder's overshirt charges disappear when the outfit reads as workwear or streetwear and halve otherwise.
